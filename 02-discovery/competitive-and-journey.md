# Competitive Analysis & Journey Map (Module 2)

## Responses
- **Role, who are you solving for? (the specific user segment or profile):** Persona 1: Lena, the payday checker

Role: Lena, 32, Cologne, is a salaried employee on a middling income with no real cushion, who shops almost entirely on her phone and uses whichever BNPL option appears at checkout.
- **Goal, what is this user ultimately trying to achieve?:** Goal: She wants the money to leave her account only after she has decided to keep an item (N-01), and to know at a glance what she owes and when.
- **Friction, the main barrier (moment of misery) stopping them from succeeding:** Friction: Her card balance and her regular pay later orders sit on two different screens, and two reminders for different amounts arrive in the same week (N-07, T-103, T-104), which confirms her fear that a card is “one more thing to check before payday” (N-02).
- **External tools, the outside platforms or tools the user is forced to use:** The research names only competing apps, so the list is short. Lena already has Klarna and PayPal installed and uses whichever appears at checkout (the brief), and N-02 says she already has two BNPL apps. Her bank card and debit card are her fallback in shops (N-03, N-06). Spreadsheets, calendars and screenshots are not in the data, so I haven’t listed them. Treat them as hypotheses to ask about in interviews.
- **The process, the 3 to 5 manual steps the user takes to get the job done:** All five steps come from the notes and tickets, but they come from different participants, so this is a composite journey and not one person’s record.

She checks her BNPL apps before payday to see what is due (N-02).
She opens the Riverty card app and finds the card balance and her regular pay later orders on two different screens, and has to work out which total is real (N-07, T-103).
She receives two reminders in the same week for different amounts and has to decide which one to act on (T-104).
In a shop, she taps her bank card by default, or tries the Riverty card and gets declined with no reason shown (N-03, N-06, T-101).
She goes back to her debit card, or pays again with another card when a late notification makes her think the payment failed (N-06, T-108).
- **Core frustration, the exact moment the process feels most “broken”:** The most broken moment is the unexplained decline at the till. She is in public, the app gives her no reason, and she has no way to find out what went wrong. A close second is the earlier moment of not knowing which total or which reminder is real, but that one is slow and private, while the decline forces an immediate fallback.
- **The evidence, a specific quote or behavior from the research that proves this:** Quote: “The card was declined at a bakery and the app did not say why. I went back to my debit card the same week.” (N-06)
Behaviour: 48 in-store declines were logged with no reason shown (T-101). Late notifications also led participants to pay again with another card (T-108).
Supporting quote for the tracking pain: “A third card is one more thing to check before payday.” (N-02)
