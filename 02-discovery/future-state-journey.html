Lena's future-state journey with Riverty's first card

Strategy: One card, one balance, one due date, and a clear answer whenever a payment fails. Friends-and-family MVP scope for Germany.

Persona: Lena, 32, Cologne. Salaried, middling income, no real cushion. Shops almost entirely on her phone and uses whichever BNPL option appears at checkout.

Goal: Money leaves her account only after she has decided to keep an item, and she knows at a glance what she owes and when.

Friction today: Her card balance and regular pay later orders sit on two screens, and two reminders for different amounts arrive in one week. A card feels like one more thing to check before payday.

Three advantages over the manual workaround

  1. One source of truth Checks one screen → no more adding up totals across apps and screens. N-02, T-103
  2. Explained failures Reads why a payment failed → stays on the card instead of reverting to debit. N-06, T-101
  3. Same tap as her bank card Pays from the phone wallet → no extra habit to remember in shops. N-03, B2

Limits to state when presenting this map:

  • The data pack is fictional practice data, and the persona combines quotes from different participants.
  • Feelings are hypotheses, and the manual workaround is inferred: the research does not document spreadsheets, screenshots or calendar reminders.
  • The advantages are design intentions from backlog items B1, B2, B3 and B8. No test has shown they change behaviour.
  • Not covered: late purchase notifications (T-108), repeated ID photo request (T-106) and the in-store pay now or later choice (B4), which is unproven and needs its own approval.
  • The test group is mostly colleagues, so real customers may behave differently.
