Slide 5 · Strategy

Strategy

Problem statement hook · Value proposition · Data-backed hypothesis

Problem statement hook

We must solve the risk that Riverty’s first card becomes one more thing heavy BNPL users have to check before payday, and drops out of their habit, by addressing the pain of working out what she owes and when across separate screens, mismatched reminders and an unexplained decline.

Value proposition

Riverty’s first consumer card gives Lena one place to see what she owes on the card and on her pay later orders, with one total she can trust, so it stops being one more thing to check before payday. It matters because heavy BNPL users carry the most open obligations (5.82 purchases a month, instalments on 25.4% of purchases), and today they piece the picture together from two screens (N-07, T-103). With one view, the card is more likely to stay in use after the first month. Month-2 activity is 51.0% and 57.1% today, with a 62% target.

It is worth building now because it is a low-effort fix (effort 2) that doesn’t change terms. The larger changes, such as due dates and pay-later control in store, need months of approval (N-11).

Data-backed hypothesis

Based on N-07 (“I could not see in one place what I owe on the card and what I owe on my normal pay later orders”) and on cohort activity falling from 77.6% in month 0 to 51.0% in month 2 for the Month 1 cohort, I believe that giving Lena, a heavy BNPL user, one balance, one due date and a plain reason whenever a payment fails will keep her using the card after her first month. I will measure this as an 8-point (about 15%) rise in month-2 activity, from a baseline of about 54% (the 51.0% and 57.1% cohorts) to 62%. I will protect partner merchant share of card volume at or above 58.8%, and will make a go/no-go decision once two consecutive cohorts of at least 150 activated cards have been observed through month 2.

Slide 6 · Research

Research

Competitive analysis / workaround summary · Journey map (key moments)

Competitive analysis / workaround · Persona 1: Lena, the payday checker

Role. Lena, 32, Cologne, is a salaried employee on a middling income with no real cushion, who shops almost entirely on her phone and uses whichever BNPL option appears at checkout.

Goal. She wants the money to leave her account only after she has decided to keep an item (N-01), and to know at a glance what she owes and when.

Friction. Her card balance and her regular pay later orders sit on two different screens, and two reminders for different amounts arrive in the same week (N-07, T-103, T-104), which confirms her fear that a card is “one more thing to check before payday” (N-02).

External tools. Lena already has Klarna and PayPal installed and uses whichever appears at checkout (the brief), and N-02 says she already has two BNPL apps. Her bank card and debit card are her fallback in shops (N-03, N-06).

The process · composite of different participants
  1. She checks her BNPL apps before payday to see what is due (N-02).
  2. She opens the Riverty card app and finds the card balance and her regular pay later orders on two different screens, and has to work out which total is real (N-07, T-103).
  3. She receives two reminders in the same week for different amounts and has to decide which one to act on (T-104).
  4. In a shop, she taps her bank card by default, or tries the Riverty card and gets declined with no reason shown (N-03, N-06, T-101).
  5. She goes back to her debit card, or pays again with another card when a late notification makes her think the payment failed (N-06, T-108).
The evidence

“The card was declined at a bakery and the app did not say why. I went back to my debit card the same week.” (N-06) · “A third card is one more thing to check before payday.” (N-02)

PRD highlights · the “Now” feature I’m scoping

B3, one balance view for card and regular pay later orders.

  • One screen showing the card balance and the open pay later orders, labelled “Card” and “Pay later orders”.
  • One headline total across both.
  • No partial totals. If either part fails to load, show which one failed and no total.
  • One entry point from the app home.

What the PRD makes explicit: the total disappears if either part fails to load.

Slide 8 · Validation

Validation

The experiment plan

Hypothesis

I believe that putting a “What you owe” view, one total across the card balance and pay later orders, on the home screen for newly activated card holders like Lena, a heavy BNPL user, will result in more of them still making a purchase two months after activation, as measured by an 8 percentage point (about 15% relative) rise in month-2 activity, from about 54% to 62%, within about 6 to 7 months. That is about 4 months of enrolment to reach 597 cards per arm, plus 2 to 3 months of follow-up. We will protect partner merchant share of card volume (63.1% in Month 4, floor 58.8%) throughout the test.

Control (A)

Today’s experience, with the card balance and pay later orders on two separate screens and no combined total. No other change ships to either arm during the test.

Variant (B) · the single change

A “What you owe” section on the app home screen, visible without scrolling: a “Total owed” figure, a row “Card” and a row “Pay later orders”. The total exists on the page only when both parts have loaded. A failed part shows “Couldn’t load”, and the other part still shows its amount.

if month-2 activity improves by at least 8 percentage points (about 54% to 62% or more) at p < 0.05, two-sided, and partner merchant share of card volume does not reach its boundary after about 6 to 7 months. The boundary is below 58.8% in any calendar month, or more than 3 points below control in the same month.

if the primary metric shows no improvement or moves negatively, meaning a lift below +2 points.

Slide 10 · Story

Story

Friction points · Aha moment · Key takeaways and what I would do next

Friction points

The hardest challenge was connecting the pain I had found to a number that could prove it. The interviews and tickets gave me a clear moment of misery for Lena: the card balance and her pay later orders on two screens, and two reminders for different amounts in the same week (N-07, T-103, T-104). The data pack measured usage, not pain. Month-2 activity falls from 77.6% to 51.0% for the Month 1 cohort, but that could be novelty fading (N-12) as easily as tracking friction. The test group was also mostly employees (253 of 431 activated cards), and my persona was a composite of different participants. So I could name the problem but not prove its size.

The second challenge was scope. My M3 hypothesis bundled three fixes: one balance, one due date and a failure reason. The feature I scoped, B3, delivers only the first, so the test I could actually run was narrower than the idea I believed in.

Aha moment

My aha came when I pressure-tested my own experiment brief. I had written an 8-point goal and a clean decision rule, then checked whether the data could ever deliver it. It could not. At the pilot’s pace of about 108 activations a month, reaching 597 cards per arm would take about 11 months of enrolment. My kill rule, a lift under +2 points, would also have rejected a real +4 point win about a quarter of the time.

A decision rule has to survive its own sample size. I rewrote it with a void check, an explicit inconclusive outcome, a kill line based on the confidence interval and a named owner, all committed before any data arrives.

Key takeaways
  • Plausible is not evidence. “Cash-flow control” and “Merchant offers” sounded like sensible features, and an AI-written persona arrived with traits like “terminal panic” that no participant said. Each one fell apart when I asked which quote or number supported it.
  • AI drafts fast and sounds equally confident about guesses. It was most useful when I gave it a structure and pushed back, and least reliable when it filled in numbers on its own.
  • Prioritisation depends on the metric you choose. I lowered B4’s value once I saw that nothing linked it to month-2 activity. The decline fix (B1) moved up when the data showed about half of declines were online, not in store.
  • Building exposes the logic of a plan. The prototype showed that my PRD’s “one tap to open” rule contradicted Lena’s complaint about having one more thing to check, so I moved the view onto the home page.
  • Most of my numbers were assumptions. The 62% target, the 58.8% guardrail floor, the 150-card cohort minimum and every effort score were mine, not the data pack’s. Labelling them kept the plan honest.
What I would do next

Likely post-launch decision: iterate. At plausible true lifts of 4 to 5 points, the test has only 29% to 42% power, so an inconclusive read is more likely than a clear ship or kill. Keep the section live, since it is cheap and low risk, and judge the next step (B8) on 90-day cohorts.
